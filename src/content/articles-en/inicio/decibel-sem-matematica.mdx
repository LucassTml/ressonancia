## A ruler for comparing

The ear copes with an enormous range: the loudest sound we can stand carries trillions of times more energy than the faintest we can detect. Writing that in ordinary numbers would be impractical, and the **decibel** solves the problem by compressing that scale into small numbers, from about 0 to 120.

A decibel is always a **comparison**: how much stronger one sound is than another. When a dB number appears on its own, there's a hidden reference. In dB SPL, the reference is the limit of human hearing, which is why 0 dB SPL doesn't mean silence. It means the faintest sound a young ear typically detects.

## How much each difference is

| Difference | What you notice |
| --- | --- |
| 1 dB | Right at the limit of what's noticeable, when it covers a broad range |
| 3 dB | A clear difference, but not a dramatic one |
| 6 dB | Much stronger; the electrical signal has doubled |
| 10 dB | Sounds about twice as loud |
| 20 dB | A huge change, like going from a quiet room to a lively conversation |

## How this shows up in headphones

A headphone graph has decibels on the vertical axis. A 3 dB boost across a broad bass region is easy to notice; 6 dB changes the headphone's character completely.

Width matters as much as height. A narrow 10 dB peak can go almost unnoticed, while a 2 dB rise spanning several octaves changes the overall impression.

<Callout type="atencao" title="About volume and hearing">
The health rule works in decibels: up to 8 hours at 85 dB is the limit recommended by NIOSH, and every 3 dB above that halves the safe time. At 100 dB, it's about 15 minutes. Since 3 dB doesn't feel like much, it's easy to go over the limit without noticing. More in [Hearing health](/percepcao/saude-auditiva).
</Callout>
