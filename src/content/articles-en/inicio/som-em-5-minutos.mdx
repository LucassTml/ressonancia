## Sound is air being pushed

When someone claps, their hands push the air around them. That air pushes the air next to it, which pushes the next, and the push travels across the room until it reaches your eardrum. The air itself barely goes anywhere. What travels is the disturbance, like a wave in a stadium: each person only stands up and sits down, yet the wave goes all the way around.

<Diagram id="air-molecules" />

Headphones do exactly the same thing in miniature. Inside them is a small part that moves back and forth, pushing the little bit of air between it and your eardrum. Everything else in this subject, from expensive cables to complicated graphs, is about controlling that movement well.

## The two questions

Every sound wave answers two questions:

- **How quickly does it go back and forth?** That is **frequency**. Slow movement sounds low; fast movement sounds high.
- **How hard does it push?** That is **amplitude**. A bigger push sounds louder.

## So why is music so complex?

A voice or a guitar doesn't produce one smooth, simple wave like the one in the experiment above. It produces many waves at once, added together. A whole song is the sum of thousands of them, changing all the time.

Even so, your headphone only does one thing: it moves a small surface back and forth, following the sum of everything. Your ear does the reverse. It splits that sum into pieces and gives your brain the sensation of "bass here, voice there, cymbal up high."

<Callout type="chave" title="The idea to keep">
A good headphone is one that moves the air the right way at every frequency, in the right proportions. Nearly every debate about headphones is really about what "the right proportions" means.
</Callout>
