<Lead>
A frequency response graph looks intimidating, but it answers a single question: at each frequency, how much sound does this headphone make compared with the others? You can read one in five steps.
</Lead>

<Diagram id="fr-anatomy" />

## 1. Look at the bottom axis: frequencies

From left to right, it runs from low to high, usually 20 Hz to 20 kHz. The ruler is in [octaves](/inicio/o-que-e-uma-oitava), which is why 100 Hz, 1 kHz and 10 kHz are evenly spaced.

## 2. Look at the side axis: level

In decibels, with about 5 or 10 dB between lines. Curves are usually aligned to read 0 dB at 1 kHz. The absolute number doesn't matter much. What counts is the **shape**: how far one region sits above or below another.

## 3. Don't expect a straight line

This is the point that confuses almost everyone. A headphone with a perfectly flat response would sound muffled and odd. That's because sound from the real world reaches your eardrum after passing through your outer ear and ear canal, which boost around 3 kHz by about 10 to 20 dB. A headphone sitting inside the canal needs to imitate that boost. It's the "mountain" you see on almost every graph.

That's why a curve is compared with a **target**, a reference curve built from preference tests, rather than with a straight line. The best known is [Harman](/resposta/targets-e-harman).

## 4. Compare with the target by region

See how far the curve strays from the target in each region, and translate:

| Curve relative to target | How it tends to sound |
| --- | --- |
| Bass above | Full and powerful; too much and it's muffled |
| Bass below | Clean but thin |
| Presence above (2 to 5 kHz) | Clear; too much and it's shrill and tiring |
| Presence below | Relaxed; too much and it's distant and lifeless |
| Treble above (5 to 10 kHz) | Bright and detailed; too much and it's sibilant |
| Treble below | Smooth and dark |

## 5. Know what the graph doesn't show

Above 8 kHz, measurements depend heavily on the equipment and on how the headphone is positioned and, above all, on your own ear. Nobody should take a narrow peak in that region very seriously.

A graph is also from **one unit**, measured on **one rig**, with **one eartip**. The seal in your ear can change the bass by several decibels.

<Callout type="chave" title="Rule of thumb">
Look at the broad trends first, an octave or wider. Thin peaks matter less, and the right-hand third of the graph is the least reliable.
</Callout>
