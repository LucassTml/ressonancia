## Doubled means up an octave

Take a 220 Hz note. Twice that is 440 Hz: one octave up. Twice that is 880 Hz: two octaves up. Half of it, 110 Hz, is one octave down. That's all.

Now listen. First 220 Hz, then 440 Hz: <Listen label="Two notes, one octave" f="220,440" wave="triangle" />.

The second note is clearly higher, and yet it feels like **the same note**. You aren't imagining it. It's one of the strongest effects in music perception, and it's why men and women singing the same melody an octave apart sound like they're singing "the same thing."

Compare with a note that isn't an octave: <Listen label="Another note" f="220,311" wave="triangle" />. You can tell that these are now two different notes.

## Where the name comes from

"Octave" comes from music. In the scale you already know (Do, Re, Mi, Fa, Sol, La, Ti) the eighth note is another Do, and the count starts again. On a keyboard, it's the pattern of keys that repeats.

<Diagram id="octave-keyboard" />

If you count the black keys too, an octave has **twelve steps**, called semitones. Every song you've ever heard in the Western tradition is built from those twelve steps, repeated in octaves up and down.

## Why this matters for headphones

Here is the point. To your ear, **going from 100 to 200 Hz is the same distance as going from 5,000 to 10,000 Hz**. Both are "one octave." The ear doesn't count hertz. It counts multiplications.

That's why:

- There are **about ten octaves** between 20 Hz and 20 kHz, and each one is an equally important part of what you hear.
- The bass, at only a few hundred hertz, is as "big" to the ear as the thousands of hertz of treble.
- Headphone graphs use a **logarithmic** ruler, which is just a name for a ruler where each octave takes up the same space.

<Diagram id="log-ruler" />

On an ordinary ruler where each hertz takes up the same space, the bass would be squashed into a corner, and nearly the whole graph would be spent on the highest octaves, the ones with the least musical content. Compare both rulers in the lab above.

## "1/3 octave" and other expressions

You'll come across phrases like "1/3-octave smoothing" or "a filter one octave wide." They just say which piece of the ruler is being considered: 1/3 of an octave is a third of a doubling, which works out to about four semitones.

<Callout type="simples">
Doubling the frequency goes up an octave. The ear treats each octave as a step of the same size, whether in the bass or the treble. That's all you need to read the bottom axis of any audio graph.
</Callout>
