## A note never comes alone

When a string vibrates, it doesn't swing in just one way. It vibrates as a whole, and at the same time in two halves, in three thirds, in four quarters. Each of those modes makes its own sound, at a multiple of the main note's frequency.

<Diagram id="string-harmonics" />

When you play a 220 Hz A, you're also producing 440, 660, 880 Hz and so on, each weaker than the last. The brain fuses all of it into one note, the 220 Hz one. The **proportion** between those components is what you recognize as guitar, flute or voice. We call that **timbre**.

## What this has to do with headphones

Two instruments playing the same note have harmonics in similar places but in different proportions. When a headphone raises or lowers one region too much while reproducing them, it changes those proportions, and the guitar starts to sound less like a guitar.

Three practical consequences follow:

- **A headphone doesn't need to play the low note for you to hear it.** The brain deduces the note from the harmonics. That's how you can make out a bass line on a phone speaker.
- **The presence and treble regions carry the identity of sounds.** A guitar's attack and the difference between consonants live in the harmonics.
- **Separating instruments depends on balance.** When one region covers up its neighbor, the ear loses the clues that tell one instrument from another. That's called [masking](/percepcao/mascaramento).

<Callout type="chave" title="The idea to keep">
Pitch comes from the main note. Timbre comes from the mix of harmonics. When someone says a headphone has "natural timbre," they mean it preserves that mix.
</Callout>
