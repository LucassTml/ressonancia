<Lead>
A transducer converts one form of energy into another. In a headphone, it turns an electrical current that varies over time into mechanical movement, and that movement into a variation in pressure. Behind dozens of technologies with marketing names, there are only a few physical principles, and understanding them says more about a headphone's sound than any driver count.
</Lead>

<Callout type="simples">
A driver is a tiny motor that pushes air back and forth following the electrical signal. A surprise: in a sealed earphone, deep bass doesn't take a big movement, only a closed space. It's like pressing the plunger of a capped syringe. That's why a tiny IEM can reach 20 Hz, and why any air leak kills the bass.
</Callout>

## From current to force

Most headphone drivers use **electromagnetism**. A conductor carrying a current $i$ in a magnetic field of density $B$ experiences a force perpendicular to both, the **Lorentz force**. For a wire of length $l$ perpendicular to the field:

<Formula say="The force on the wire grows with the magnet's strength, the amount of wire in the field and the current. Double the current and you double the force.">

$$
F = B \cdot l \cdot i
$$

<Vars
  items={[
    ['F', 'force on the conductor, in newtons'],
    ['B', 'magnetic flux density where the wire is, in tesla'],
    ['l', 'length of wire in the field, in meters'],
    ['i', 'current, in amperes'],
  ]}
/>

</Formula>

The product $Bl$ is the motor's **force factor**: how much force you get per ampere. Stronger magnets (neodymium) and more wire in the field increase $Bl$, improving sensitivity and control of the movement. The beauty of the equation is its **linearity**: doubling the current doubles the force. As long as $B$ and $l$ remain constant throughout the movement, the motor doesn't distort.

**Dynamic** (moving-coil) and **planar magnetic** drivers use this force directly. **Balanced armatures** use a variant: the current magnetizes a piece of iron, which is attracted by magnets. **Electrostatics** swap magnetism for forces between electric charges, and **piezoelectrics** use materials that deform with voltage. Each principle has its own article in this module.

## From force to movement: the mass-spring system

The moving part of any driver (diaphragm, coil, membrane) has **mass** $m$. It's held by a suspension, which acts as a **spring** of stiffness $k$ (or, equivalently, compliance $C = 1/k$). And the movement suffers **losses** (internal friction of materials, resistance of air passing through openings) that act as a damper of mechanical resistance $R$. The equation of motion is that of a forced oscillator:

<Formula say="The moving part behaves like a mass on a spring with a brake: three effects together resist the force applied.">

$$
m\,\ddot{x} + R\,\dot{x} + k\,x = F(t)
$$

</Formula>

This system has a **resonant frequency**:

<Formula say="A stiffer spring raises the resonant frequency; a heavier mass lowers it.">

$$
f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}
$$

</Formula>

and a **Q factor** describing how pronounced the resonance is, $Q = \sqrt{km}/R$. High Q means little damping: a tall, narrow peak at the resonance and a slow decay. Low Q means heavy damping: a smooth response with no peak.

The behavior changes radically depending on the frequency relative to $f_0$:

- **Below $f_0$:** the spring dominates. Displacement is proportional to force and practically constant with frequency.
- **Near $f_0$:** mass and spring exchange energy easily; movement is amplified by Q.
- **Above $f_0$:** the mass dominates. Displacement falls 12 dB per octave, but **acceleration** remains constant.

## From movement to pressure: it depends on where the air is

Here's the part that often surprises. The relationship between the diaphragm's movement and sound pressure depends on the **acoustic environment** it works in.

### Free field: pressure proportional to acceleration

A small membrane radiating into open space (a speaker in a room, from a distance) produces, at a distance, a pressure proportional to the diaphragm's **acceleration**. Since acceleration is constant above $f_0$, a speaker has a flat response **above** its resonance. That's why woofers are designed with low resonances (tens of hertz) and need to displace huge volumes of air to reproduce bass: to keep the pressure constant, each octave down requires four times the displacement.

### Small sealed chamber: pressure proportional to displacement

A well-sealed IEM forms, with the ear canal, a closed cavity of about 1–2 cm³. For frequencies whose wavelength is much larger than that cavity, all the bass and mids, the pressure inside it rises and falls uniformly, as in a capped syringe. By the gas law (adiabatic compression), the pressure change is proportional to the change in **volume**, that is, to the diaphragm's **displacement**:

<Formula say="In a small sealed space, pressure simply follows how far the diaphragm moves, at every low frequency.">

$$
p \approx \frac{\gamma P_0}{V}\,\Delta V = \frac{\rho c^2}{V}\, S_d\, x
$$

<Vars
  items={[
    ['V', 'cavity volume (residual canal + front volume of the earphone)'],
    ['S_d', 'effective diaphragm area'],
    ['x', 'diaphragm displacement'],
    ['ρc²', 'adiabatic bulk modulus of air (≈ 1.42 × 10⁵ Pa)'],
  ]}
/>

</Formula>

Since displacement is constant **below** the resonance, a driver in a sealed chamber has a flat response **below** its resonance, in principle down to arbitrarily low frequencies, as long as the seal holds. This inverts the logic of speakers: an IEM driver can have its resonance at several kilohertz and still reproduce 20 Hz with ease.

It's worth doing the math. To produce 100 dB SPL (2 Pa RMS) in a 2 cm³ cavity with an 8 mm diaphragm (area ≈ 50 mm²), the displacement needed is on the order of

<Formula say="For a loud 100 dB in an IEM, the diaphragm only needs to move about 0.6 micrometers, whether at 20 Hz or 200 Hz.">

$$
x \approx \frac{p\,V}{\rho c^2 S_d} = \frac{2 \cdot 2\times10^{-6}}{1.42\times10^{5} \cdot 5\times10^{-5}} \approx 0.6\ \mu\text{m}
$$

</Formula>

Less than a micrometer, and that holds for 20 Hz as for 200 Hz. A 16 cm speaker in a room would need millimeters of excursion for the same level at 20 Hz. That's why tiny IEMs have deep bass, and why **any leak** destroys it: with an air outlet, the cavity stops being closed and low-frequency pressure escapes.

<Callout type="chave" title="The physics of IEM bass">
In sealed IEMs, bass doesn't depend on moving a lot of air but on **keeping the cavity closed**. An IEM's bass extension is limited mainly by the seal and venting, not by the size of the driver. A large dynamic driver helps generate bass with less distortion and more easily when there's venting, but it isn't why the bass exists.
</Callout>

## Above the resonance, and modes

If an IEM is flat below the driver's resonance, why not make all of them with very high resonances? Because the real world complicates things:

- **The cavity stops being "small"** above a few kilohertz: the canal starts having standing waves (see [pinna gain](/percepcao/pinna-gain)), and pressure is no longer uniform.
- **The diaphragm stops being rigid.** At high frequencies, different parts of the diaphragm vibrate at different phases (**breakup modes**), creating peaks and dips.
- **Tubes and internal cavities** have their own resonances.
- **The headphone must produce presence gain** (the artificial pinna gain), which requires shaping the response, not just leaving it flat.

That's why designing a headphone is always an **acoustic** project around the driver: chambers, tubes, vents, filters and sometimes several drivers with a crossover. That's the subject of [acoustic construction](/drivers/construcao-acustica) and [tuning](/iems/tuning).

## Distortion: when linearity is lost

Everything above assumes linearity. In practice, several things become nonlinear at large displacements:

- **$Bl$ varies with position:** if the coil leaves the region of uniform field, force per ampere decreases.
- **The suspension stiffens:** the spring stops being linear at large excursions.
- **Compressed air isn't perfectly linear** at very high pressures (irrelevant at normal levels).
- **Materials have hysteresis** and nonlinear behavior.

These nonlinearities generate [harmonic and intermodulation distortion](/eletronica/medidas-de-desempenho), more pronounced in the bass (where displacement is largest, especially with venting) and at high levels. In well-designed IEMs, distortion at normal listening levels is usually very low, often below 1% even in the bass, and much lower in the mids.

## Electrical impedance: the amplifier's side

From the amplifier's point of view, the driver is an **electrical load** with an impedance that varies with frequency. In a dynamic driver, it's dominated by the resistance of the coil wire, with a peak at the mechanical resonance (the movement generates a back-electromotive force) and a gentle rise in the treble from inductance. In balanced armatures, inductance is much larger and impedance rises a lot with frequency. In IEMs with several drivers and a crossover, the impedance curve can vary hugely, making the sound sensitive to the source's [output impedance](/eletronica/potencia-e-sensibilidade).
