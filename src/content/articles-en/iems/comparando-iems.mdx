## The four traps

**1. Volume.** The IEM that plays louder tends to seem better: more bass, more detail, more presence. Two IEMs with different sensitivities, connected to the same source at the same volume, can differ by 5 to 10 dB. And differences of just 0.5 to 1 dB, too small to be perceived as "louder," are already enough to bias judgment. Try it in the lab below: the same audio, with a hidden level difference.

**2. Fit.** Each IEM has its own shell and nozzle shape; with the same eartips, each may seal and insert differently. The bass difference caused by a poorer fit can easily exceed the difference between the two IEMs. Ensure the seal of each, with eartips suited to each.

**3. Short auditory memory.** Memory for timbre details lasts a few seconds. Comparisons with minutes in between compare memories, not sounds. Switch quickly and use short excerpts.

**4. Expectation.** Knowing which IEM is the expensive one, the new one, the praised one or the one you just bought changes what you hear. This effect is real and powerful (see [bias and expectation](/ciencia/vies-e-placebo)), and the only defense is not knowing which is which.

## A practical protocol

1. **Match the volume.** Ideally, measure: with a coupler and an SPL meter, or at least with a microphone positioned the same way, playing pink noise. Without measuring, use the difference in sensitivity in dB/V as an estimate (the calculator helps) and adjust precisely. The goal is a difference under ~0.2 dB.
2. **Ensure the seal** of both, each with its ideal eartips.
3. **Pick 3 to 5 short excerpts** (10–20 seconds) you know very well, with different characteristics: voice, acoustic instruments, bass, many simultaneous instruments.
4. **Switch several times** on each excerpt. Note what you perceive as a difference, not "which is better."
5. **Whenever possible, do it blind.** Ask someone to hand you the IEMs without saying which is which (with similar shells, or with your eyes closed), or use an EQ comparison: equalize a single IEM to imitate the response of the other and compare blind in software, the same principle as the virtual headphone method.
6. **Repeat on another day.** Impressions that hold up are worth more.

To test whether **any audible difference** exists (for example, between two sources, two cables or two files), the suitable method is the **ABX test**, with statistics, described in [blind tests and ABX](/ciencia/testes-cegos-e-abx).

## Marketing and pseudoscience: warning signs

Some claims appear frequently and deserve skepticism:

- **"More drivers = more detail."** Driver count doesn't determine quality.
- **"A cable that opens up the stage / improves the bass."** No plausible electrical mechanism at the lengths and currents involved; see [cables](/eletronica/conexoes-e-cadeia).
- **"200 hours of burn-in."** Measurable changes in drivers after use, when they exist, are small and occur in the first hours. Listener adaptation to the new sound is a more likely explanation for most reports.
- **"Response from 5 Hz to 50 kHz."** Without a tolerance it means nothing; and above 20 kHz, it's not audible.
- **"Patented technology that eliminates distortion / creates 3D sound."** Ask for measurements.
- **"Only trained ears can tell."** Trained ears perceive better what exists, and are the first to pass an ABX when the difference is real.
- **Comparisons without matched volume**, in videos or shops, with the most expensive product always playing louder.

None of this means every extraordinary claim is false. It means extraordinary claims require proportionate evidence, and that good evidence in audio is relatively easy to produce: a measurement and a well-done blind test.
