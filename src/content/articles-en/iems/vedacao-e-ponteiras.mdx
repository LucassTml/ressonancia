## Seal: the condition for bass to exist

<Diagram id="seal" />

As we saw in [transducers](/drivers/transdutores), an IEM produces bass by compressing the air of a closed cavity. If there's a gap, the air escapes through it at low frequencies, where movement is slow and the air has time to flow, and the pressure drops. Acoustically, the gap acts as a **high-pass filter**: below a cutoff frequency, the bass plummets. The larger the gap, the higher the cutoff frequency.

The lab below shows the order of magnitude: a small leak you barely notice takes several decibels off the sub-bass; a moderate leak makes the IEM lose the body of its bass.

<Lab id="fit" />

**How to know if it's sealed:** play music with constant bass and press the IEM lightly inward. If the bass increases noticeably, the seal wasn't good. Another sign: your own voice gets deeper and more muffled when the seal is good (the occlusion effect). Also try talking or opening your mouth: canals that change shape with the jaw can break the seal.

## Size and shape

Ear canals vary in diameter (typically 6 to 9 mm), shape (round or oval) and curvature, and a person's two ears may need different sizes. The right eartip is the **smallest that seals well**: too large is uncomfortable and can deform, pushing the IEM out; too small leaks.

Shapes matter too: longer eartips insert deeper; "double" or "triple flange" tips seal at more than one point and reach deep insertions; shallow, wide tips leave the nozzle closer to the entrance of the canal.

## Silicone vs foam

**Silicone** is durable, washable and keeps its shape. The seal depends a lot on getting the right size. Softer silicones adapt better to oval canals; firmer ones keep the IEM more stable.

**Foam** (memory foam, such as polyurethane) is compressed before insertion and expands in the canal, adapting to its shape. It usually seals better, isolates more and reduces microphonics, but it wears out in weeks or months, absorbs dirt and needs to be replaced. Acoustically, foam slightly damps the treble (it absorbs some high-frequency energy and reduces resonances), which some people prefer with bright IEMs.

There are also **hybrid** tips (silicone with a foam core), **high-grip silicone** tips and **custom** molds made from an impression of the canal. The latter offer the most consistent seal but require an audiologist or technician to make the impression and only fit compatible IEMs.

## Bore diameter

The eartip's hole (*bore*) is the continuation of the IEM's nozzle. It's a small acoustic tube, and its diameter mainly affects the treble:

- **Wide bore**: less resistance and inertance in the sound path; it tends to preserve or increase the treble and "air."
- **Narrow bore**: acts as a slight acoustic filter; it tends to reduce the treble and, by contrast, leave the bass and lower mids relatively more present.

The differences are usually 1 to 4 dB above ~5 kHz, noticeable especially in IEMs already at the limit of brightness.

## Insertion depth

Insertion depth changes the length of the residual canal between the eartip and the eardrum, and with it the occluded canal's **half-wave resonance** (see [pinna gain](/percepcao/pinna-gain)). Deeper insertions shorten the residual canal and push that resonance up; shallow insertions bring it down. In practice, this shifts a peak or dip in the 6 to 10 kHz region by more than 1 kHz.

That explains why:

- the same IEM can sound sibilant with one eartip and smooth with another;
- reviews diverge so much about treble;
- coupler measurements vary above 6 kHz according to the depth used by the reviewer.

Deep insertion also reduces the occlusion effect (the eartip rests on the bony part of the canal) and increases isolation.

## A method for choosing eartips

1. Start with the size that comes installed. Do the pressure test (does the bass grow when you push?).
2. Try one size up and one size down, in each ear separately.
3. Once the seal is stable, compare **types** (wide vs narrow bore, silicone vs foam) with music you know well, paying attention to sibilance and brightness.
4. Remember that the choice of eartips alters the response: if you use EQ, adjust it **after** settling on the eartips.

<Callout type="mito" title="&quot;This IEM has no bass at all&quot;">
Before concluding that, check the seal. The vast majority of complaints about "lack of bass" in well-measured IEMs come from the wrong eartip size. Coupler measurements are made with a perfect seal; your ear, not always.
</Callout>
