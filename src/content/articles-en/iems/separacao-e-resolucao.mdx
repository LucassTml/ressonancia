But the question behind them is concrete: what does a headphone need so that I can tell each instrument apart, even when several are playing in the same midrange region? The answer begins in the brain and ends in well-known measurements.

Your brain does the separating, using clues in the sound: which notes start together, which belong to the same instrument, which come from the same place. A headphone just has to keep those clues intact, which mostly means a balanced response, low distortion, well-matched channels and a good seal.

## The brain separates the instruments

A headphone doesn't "separate" anything: it delivers to the eardrum a single pressure varying over time, the sum of all the instruments. What breaks that sum into sources is the auditory system, in a process Albert Bregman called **auditory scene analysis**. For this, the brain uses clues that exist in the signal itself:

- **Harmonicity:** components that are multiples of the same fundamental tend to be grouped as a single source. A slightly mistuned harmonic "pops out" of the group and is heard separately.
- **Onset synchrony:** components that start together belong to the same event. A component that starts a few milliseconds earlier tends to be heard as another source.
- **Common fate:** components that vary together, with the same vibrato or the same crescendo, are grouped.
- **Continuity of timbre and pitch:** similar successive notes are linked into one melodic "stream."
- **Spatial position:** components with the same interaural time and level differences are assigned to the same direction.

The lab demonstrates the first three cues with a complex sound whose components are all between 200 Hz and 2.4 kHz, in other words, within the mids.

<Lab id="mistuned" />

That answers the first part of the question: **instruments in the same region aren't distinguished by frequency but by patterns**: of harmonics, attacks, variations and position. A violin and a viola playing together occupy practically the same band; you distinguish them because each has its own harmonic series, bow attacks, vibrato and position in the stereo field.

## What the headphone needs to preserve

The headphone's job, then, is **not to destroy those clues**. That translates into precise physical requirements.

### 1. A balanced, smooth frequency response

The most important factor. Two reasons:

- **Masking.** A broad excess region hides neighboring regions, especially above it (the [upward spread](/percepcao/mascaramento)). Elevated mid-bass hides detail from 300 Hz to 1.5 kHz; exaggerated presence can cover nuances above it. In a balanced headphone, each region has a chance to be heard.
- **Timbre integrity.** The cues of harmonicity and timbre depend on the **relative** amplitudes of each instrument's harmonics. A pronounced peak or dip in the mids alters those relationships equally for all instruments, bringing their timbres closer and making them harder to distinguish. A smooth response preserves the differences between them.

### 2. Enough presence and treble

**Attacks**, the start of each note, the bite of a bow, the strike of a pick, concentrate energy between 2 and 8 kHz. They're the basis of the onset synchrony cue. A headphone that's too dark "softens" attacks and makes it harder to perceive where each note begins; it's one reason bright headphones are associated with "resolution." The balance is delicate: too much brightness also masks and tires.

### 3. Low distortion, especially intermodulation

Distortion creates components that weren't in the music. Low-order **harmonic distortion** adds components that fit into the existing harmonic series and is relatively benign. **Intermodulation** (IMD) is more damaging to separation: with several instruments playing, a nonlinear system generates products at sums and differences of frequencies that don't belong to any harmonic series, "orphan" components the brain can't assign to any source and that blur the scene. This tends to occur at high levels and when strong bass demands large excursion from a driver also reproducing mids (the large movement modulates the reproduction of the mids).

In good-quality IEMs, distortion at normal levels is low enough not to be the limiting factor. It becomes relevant at high volumes, with large EQ bass boosts, or in poorly resolved drivers.

### 4. Equality between channels

The spatial cue depends on differences between the ears. If the left and right earpieces have different responses, 2 dB more on one channel at 3 kHz, for example, the instruments shift and the center image spreads. Good **channel matching** (response and phase) is what produces precise **imaging**: instruments with stable, well-defined positions between the ears.

### 5. Absence of strong resonances

In a minimum-phase system, a narrow peak in the response is also a resonance that's slow to decay. Poorly damped resonances "prolong" certain frequencies after the sound has stopped, masking the start of the next event. Again, this is in the frequency response: a headphone with a smooth response has a fast decay.

### 6. Low noise floor and a good seal

Low-level details such as reverberation, breathing and decays need to be above the hearing threshold and ambient noise. A quiet source and a good seal (which reduces external noise) keep those details audible at moderate volumes.

## What's measurable and what's impression

| Perceived attribute | Mainly explained by | Measurable? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Separation | Balanced FR (masking), low IMD, channel matching | Yes, indirectly |
| Resolution / detail | Presence and treble, no masking, low noise, low distortion | Yes, indirectly |
| Imaging | Channel matching (response and phase) | Yes |
| Soundstage | Recording, treble and 3–10 kHz region, resonances, individual HRTF | Partially |
| "Speed" / attack | FR (presence, no resonances), distortion | Yes, via FR |
| Layering | A combination of all the above | Partially |

There's no standardized measure called "resolution." But the physical components that produce these impressions are, to a large extent, known measurements. And it's worth remembering that **a headphone can't add detail** that isn't on the recording: it can only stop hiding it. Headphones that "reveal more" generally do so by having more energy in the region where details lie, which is sometimes great and sometimes only excess brightness.

## Soundstage in an IEM

As we saw in [spatial localization](/percepcao/localizacao-espacial), IEMs bypass the pinna and have no acoustic crosstalk: the image stays between the ears, inside the head. Differences in "stage" between IEMs exist as impressions and are associated with factors such as the level and smoothness of the treble, the shape of the presence region (some shapes suggest more distance), the absence of resonances and the recorded material itself (reverberation, miking). They're small compared with the difference between any IEM and a pair of speakers in a room.

<Callout type="chave" title="Answering the question">
To tell instruments apart, even in the same region, a headphone needs: (1) a balanced, smooth response, without broad excess regions that mask neighbors and without peaks that distort timbre; (2) enough presence and treble for attacks; (3) low distortion, especially intermodulation; (4) well-matched channels; (5) few resonances; (6) a good seal and a quiet source. The rest is the work of your brain, which is very good at it when it receives the clues intact.
</Callout>
