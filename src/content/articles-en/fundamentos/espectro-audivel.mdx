<Callout type="simples">
Audio engineers split sound into regions, from sub-bass you feel to treble that adds shimmer. Each region does a recognizable job: bass gives weight, mids carry voices and instruments, the presence region gives clarity. When you read a graph, you're reading how much of each region a headphone delivers.

## A map in octaves

<Diagram id="log-ruler" />

Since hearing treats frequencies in a roughly logarithmic way, the natural way to divide the spectrum is by [**octaves**](/inicio/o-que-e-uma-oitava): each time the frequency doubles, we move one unit. There are about ten octaves from 20 Hz to 20 kHz (20 → 40 → 80 → 160 → 320 → 640 → 1,280 → 2,560 → 5,120 → 10,240 → 20,480). A piano keyboard, from 27.5 Hz to 4,186 Hz, fits a little over seven; everything above ~4 kHz is harmonics, not fundamental notes.

On the map below, each octave takes up the same horizontal space. The boundaries between regions are conventions, and different authors and engineers use different numbers, but the general logic is shared. Click a region to read about it and hear only that band (or everything except it) in pink noise.

## Sub-bass (≈ 20–60 Hz): feeling more than hearing

In this region, wavelengths measure between 6 and 17 meters. The ear isn't very sensitive here: for a 30 Hz tone to sound as loud as 1 kHz at 60 dB SPL, it needs more than 30 dB extra, according to equal-loudness contours. At the same time, these frequencies excite the body (chest, abdomen, bones), and much of the experience of sub-bass at a concert is **tactile**. With headphones, that tactile part doesn't exist; only the pressure at the eardrum arrives, which partly explains why many people prefer more sub-bass in headphones than in speakers measured in a room, a point that comes up again in the research on the [Harman curve](/resposta/targets-e-harman).

Little music has relevant content below 30 Hz: pipe organs, synthesizers, some electronic kick drums, film soundtracks. Even so, clean extension to 20 Hz contributes to the sensation of "weight" and space, because it preserves the fundamental and first harmonic of very low notes.

## Mid-bass (≈ 60–250 Hz): body and impact

This is where you find the body of the kick drum (the "thump" lies between 50 and 100 Hz), the fundamentals of electric bass (41 Hz to ~400 Hz), the chest of male voices and the sensation of "warmth." This is the region that most determines whether a headphone sounds **full** or **thin**, and where preferences vary the most between people.

Too much here is the most common cause of a "boomy" sound or "fat" bass that seems slow. That impression of slowness doesn't generally come from a slow driver. It comes from the fact that a broad boost in the mid-bass **masks** the frequencies just above it (masking spreads upward, see [masking](/percepcao/mascaramento)) and stretches the perception of each bass note, which takes longer to fall out of audibility.

## Lower mids (≈ 250–800 Hz): density and weight of notes

Most fundamentals of voices and melodic instruments fall here: guitar, cello, French horn, the left hand of the piano, female voices. It's the region of a timbre's "thickness." In excess, the sound gets **muddy** and stuffy, as if coming from inside a box; if lacking, it gets **hollow** and distant. Mixing engineers often cut a few decibels in this region on instruments that don't need it, precisely to make room for the others.

## Mids (≈ 800 Hz–2 kHz): character and intelligibility

The first formants of many vowels and the "bite" of guitars and pianos live here. A raised 1 to 2 kHz region sounds **nasal** or "honky"; recessed, it pushes voices to the back of the mix. Small variations in this band are very noticeable because it concentrates information that identifies timbres.

## Upper mids and presence (≈ 2–5 kHz): the most sensitive region

This is the band where hearing is most sensitive; the hearing threshold reaches its minimum around 3 to 4 kHz. The reason is largely **mechanical**: the resonance of the ear canal and concha naturally amplifies this region by 10 to 20 dB before the sound reaches the eardrum. This is [pinna gain](/percepcao/pinna-gain). Consonants like "t," "k," "p" and "f" concentrate much of what makes speech **intelligible** here, as do instrument attacks.

This has two important practical consequences. First, a headphone needs to reproduce **some** gain in this region to sound natural, because we're used to hearing everything with that boost; taking pinna gain out of the equation makes the sound dark and distant. Second, since sensitivity is at its highest here, **excesses** are very audible and tiring: a 3 dB peak above ideal at 3 kHz is felt as harshness or shoutiness, and listening fatigue comes quickly.

## Treble (≈ 5–10 kHz): brightness, sibilance and texture

Cymbals, the texture of bowed strings, a singer's breath and the **sibilants** ("s," "sh," "x"), concentrated between 5 and 9 kHz. The ear remains sensitive here, and a headphone's response as measured on a coupler starts to depend heavily on insertion and each person's anatomy, because of the resonances of the closed canal. This is the region where two listeners with the same IEM diverge the most; see [individual perception](/percepcao/percepcao-individual).

## Air and extension (≈ 10–20 kHz)

Upper harmonics, the fine shimmer of cymbals and triangles, the sensation of "air" and space. Hearing sensitivity falls rapidly above ~12 kHz, and falls a lot with age. Even so, smooth extension in this region helps a headphone sound open rather than "closed," provided there are no narrow peaks, which tend to sound like fine roughness. It's also the hardest region to measure precisely, because of coupler limitations and insertion variation.

## Why each region sounds different

Putting the pieces together, three mechanisms explain why different frequencies produce such distinct perceptions:

1. **External acoustics.** Head, outer ear and ear canal transform sound before the eardrum, with maximum gain between 2 and 5 kHz.
2. **Mechanics of the middle ear and cochlea.** The middle ear transmits most efficiently between ~500 Hz and 4 kHz; the cochlea has wider filters (in Hz) at high frequencies and its organization roughly follows a logarithmic scale above ~500 Hz.
3. **Habit and meaning.** The frequencies that carry speech information are those to which the auditory system devotes the most resources. It's no coincidence that they're also the most sensitive.

<Callout type="chave">
**Most of music's energy is in the bass; most of its information is in the mids.** The long-term average spectrum of most recordings falls with frequency, much like pink noise. But identifying instruments, words and details depends disproportionately on the ~500 Hz to 5 kHz range. A headphone can have impressive bass and still sound confused if this region is unbalanced.
</Callout>

## Instruments don't live in just one region

Tables that associate each instrument with a frequency band are common. They're useful, but misleading if read literally. A 440 Hz violin note has significant harmonics up to 10 kHz; a kick drum with a 60 Hz fundamental has the "click" of the beater's attack between 2 and 5 kHz; a male voice with a 120 Hz fundamental has the formants that define its vowels between 300 and 3,000 Hz. That's why the bars on the map show two layers: the fundamentals (solid) and the approximate extent of relevant harmonics and noises (thin line).

This overlap explains why changes in frequency response rarely affect "just one instrument," and why mixing is the art of giving each source a place in the spectrum without destroying its timbre. It also explains why we can tell apart instruments playing in the same region: it isn't the frequency band that separates them but the **pattern** of harmonics, attacks and variations, the theme of the article on [separation and resolution](/iems/separacao-e-resolucao).

## From the map to the graph

With this map, a frequency response graph stops being an abstract line. A raised step below 200 Hz is a bass shelf (more body and impact); a wide dip between 1 and 3 kHz means recessed voices; a narrow peak at 7 kHz is a candidate for sibilance. The full vocabulary of these regions and their flaws is the subject of [regions and flaws](/resposta/regioes-e-defeitos). Before that, though, it's worth understanding what exactly makes up the music passing through all those regions: [pitch, melody and harmony](/fundamentos/melodia-e-harmonia).
