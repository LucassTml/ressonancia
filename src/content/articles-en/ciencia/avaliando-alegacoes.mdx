<Lead>
New audio claims appear every day: a cable that "opens up the stage," a DAC that "reveals hidden layers," an IEM with "technology that eliminates fatigue." Some are true, many are exaggerated, others have no basis at all. You don't need to be an engineer to evaluate them. You only need a list of questions and to know where to look for reliable information.
</Lead>

<Callout type="simples">
Before believing a claim, ask: what exactly is supposed to change, how would that happen physically, is it big enough to hear, and has anyone measured it or tested it blind? That short routine filters out most nonsense.
</Callout>

## A six-question routine

**1. What exactly is being claimed?**
"Sounds better" isn't testable. "Has 3 dB more bass below 100 Hz" is. Translate the claim into something that changes the pressure at the eardrum: frequency response, distortion, noise, difference between channels, timing.

**2. Is there a plausible physical mechanism?**
For something to change the sound, it has to change the electrical or acoustic signal measurably. A source with high output impedance changes the response of multi-BA IEMs: a clear mechanism. A 1-meter cable with 0.1 Ω of resistance changing "the texture of the mids": no known mechanism of sufficient magnitude.

**3. Is the magnitude audible?**
Even with a mechanism, the effect may be too small. Compare with the thresholds in [measurable vs audible](/ciencia/mensuravel-vs-audivel). An improvement in distortion from 0.001% to 0.0005% is real and inaudible.

**4. Are there measurements?**
From whom? Under what conditions? With what equipment? Independent measurements with a declared method are worth more than marketing graphs with no scale.

**5. Are there controlled tests?**
Does the difference survive matched volume and blind listening? If nobody has done the test, the claim is open; if it's been done and has repeatedly failed, it's unlikely.

**6. Who benefits from the claim?**
Manufacturers, shops and influencers with commercial ties aren't necessarily wrong, but they have incentives. Look for sources with no direct interest or with declared conflicts.

## Warning signs

- Vague, metaphorical language with no measurement ("more organic," "more quantum").
- Explanations invoking physics incorrectly ("more aligned electrons," "scalar waves").
- Claims that measurements "don't capture" the effect, with no proposal for how to test it.
- Systematic refusal of blind tests ("blind testing stresses the listener").
- Testimonials as the only evidence.
- Disproportionate prices justified by mysterious materials or processes.

## Reliable sources

**Research and standards**

- *Journal of the Audio Engineering Society* (JAES) and AES convention proceedings: the main technical literature in the field.
- *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* (JASA): acoustics and psychoacoustics.
- IEC (60318, 60268-7, 61672), ISO (226, 532, 7029, 11904) and ITU-R (BS.1116, BS.1534, BS.1770) standards.

**Reference books**

- Floyd Toole, *Sound Reproduction*: speakers, rooms and the science of preference.
- Brian C. J. Moore, *An Introduction to the Psychology of Hearing*: psychoacoustics.
- Zwicker and Fastl, *Psychoacoustics: Facts and Models*.
- Ken Pohlmann, *Principles of Digital Audio*.
- Douglas Self, *Audio Power Amplifier Design* and *Small Signal Audio Design*.
- Kinsler et al., *Fundamentals of Acoustics*; Beranek and Mellow, *Acoustics: Sound Fields and Transducers*.

**Open data and tools**

- Open-source projects with measurement data and target curves, such as AutoEq (the source of the targets used on this site).
- Measurement databases from reviewers who publish their methods and equipment.
- Technical forums with a culture of blind tests and measurements (for example, communities like Hydrogenaudio on the subject of codecs).

**With caution**

- Subjective reviews: useful for comfort, build and impressions; calibrate them by comparing with measurements.
- Manufacturer materials: useful for specs, rarely impartial.
- Communities: great for discovering products and sharing experiences; collective opinion isn't scientific evidence.
