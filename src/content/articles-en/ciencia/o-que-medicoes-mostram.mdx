<Lead>
Measurements are the most powerful tool for understanding a headphone, and also among the most misused. A graph can precisely reveal an IEM's tonal balance, but it can also suggest differences that don't exist, hide differences that do or be read with a confidence it doesn't support. Knowing what a measurement can and can't say is the basis of critical thinking in audio.
</Lead>

<Callout type="simples">
A good measurement is excellent for the tonal balance up to about 5 kHz. It's less reliable above that, and it comes from one unit on one artificial ear. So small differences between two graphs shouldn't be taken too seriously.
</Callout>

## What measurements show well

**Frequency response** below ~5–6 kHz, measured on a standardized coupler with a verified seal. It's reproducible, correlates strongly with the perception of tonality and, according to preference research, explains most of the differences in preference between headphones.

**Harmonic and intermodulation distortion** as a function of level and frequency. It shows whether a driver is nearing its limit at high levels, especially in the bass.

**Impedance** as a function of frequency. It predicts how the headphone will interact with high-output-impedance sources.

**Channel matching**. Differences between left and right affect imaging.

**Sensitivity**. It makes it possible to calculate how much voltage the headphone needs.

**Source performance** (DACs and amplifiers): response, noise, distortion, output impedance and maximum voltage are measured with laboratory precision by equipment such as Audio Precision's and follow standards such as AES17.

## What an isolated measurement doesn't show

### Variation between units

A measurement is of **one unit** (or a few). Each example of a model differs within manufacturing tolerances: balanced armature drivers, for example, are often specified at ±2 to 3 dB of sensitivity in certain bands. The lab below simulates a batch.

Consequence: differences of 1–2 dB between the graphs of two models, each measured on a single unit, can be within production variation and say nothing about the models.

### Repeatability of mounting

Removing and refitting the same IEM on the coupler changes the measurement, especially above 5 kHz (insertion depth) and below 100 Hz (seal). Good reviewers average several mountings; graphs from a single mounting are less reliable.

### Your ear

The coupler represents an average ear. The response at your eardrum differs, individually, mainly above 4–5 kHz. The measurement tells you about the headphone; your experience depends on the headphone **and** on you. See [individual perception](/percepcao/percepcao-individual).

### Differences between rigs and methods

Measurements on different rigs (711, clones, 5128, heads with ears) aren't directly comparable, and smoothing, normalization and compensation change the appearance. See [how a headphone is measured](/resposta/como-se-mede).

### Attributes not found in a single measurement

Comfort, weight, real isolation in your ear, durability, cable microphonics and, in part, spatial impressions don't appear on a frequency response graph.

## How to draw correct conclusions from a graph

1. **Ask where it comes from:** rig, number of units and mountings, smoothing, normalization.
2. **Compare only within the same reviewer** and the same equipment.
3. **Weight each region by its reliability:** high between 100 Hz and 5 kHz, medium below 100 Hz (seal), low above 6 kHz (insertion and anatomy).
4. **Weight by audibility:** broad deviations matter more than narrow ones; peaks more than dips.
5. **Treat small differences as uncertainty**, not fact, unless they come from averages of several units.

<Callout type="evidencia" title="Measurement and perception don't compete">
Good measurements don't replace listening, and listening doesn't refute good measurements. When a subjective impression contradicts a reliable measurement below 5 kHz, the most likely explanations lie in the listening itself (volume, fit, expectation, a different unit). When the contradiction is above 6 kHz, the listener's anatomy is a legitimate explanation. Critical thinking calls for investigating the discrepancy, not choosing a side on principle.
</Callout>
