Perception isn't a passive record of sound: it's an inference the brain makes by combining what reaches the ears with what it expects, knows and wants. That isn't a flaw of inattentive people. It's how human perception works. Understanding these mechanisms protects against wrong conclusions and also against the opposite error: dismissing all subjective experience as "placebo."
</Lead>

<Callout type="simples">
What you expect changes what you hear. It happens to everyone, experts included. That doesn't make your experience fake, but if you want to know whether the sound itself changed, you have to test without knowing which is which.

## Perception is active

The auditory system receives an ambiguous, incomplete signal and interprets it using prior knowledge. That's what makes it possible to understand speech amid noise, recognize a voice on the phone or "hear" a note that was masked for an instant. The same mechanism lets **non-auditory information** influence what is heard.

## The most relevant biases in audio

**Expectation bias.** If you expect to hear a difference, you tend to perceive it. Knowing a cable cost a lot, that a DAC is "reference grade" or that a review praised an IEM's bass changes the experience.

**Confirmation bias.** We tend to notice and remember evidence confirming what we already believe and to discard what contradicts it.

**Justifying the investment.** After spending a lot on a piece of equipment, there's strong motivation to perceive it as better; the cognitive dissonance of admitting otherwise is uncomfortable.

**Visual and brand bias.** Toole and Olive showed, in loudspeaker tests, that preferences changed when listeners saw the products: large, expensive speakers rose in the ranking, and the differences between models of very different measured performance shrank. In blind listening, preferences followed measured performance much more closely.

**Order and memory.** The first or last option heard can be favored; memory for timbre details is short, and spaced comparisons compare reconstructed memories.

**Volume.** Louder seems better, as seen in [comparing IEMs](/iems/comparando-iems). It's a perceptual bias, not a cognitive one, and it affects everyone.

**Adaptation.** After some time with a headphone, the brain recalibrates its reference. A new headphone can sound "wrong" at first and "right" weeks later, without anything in it having changed. Many reports of *burn-in* are compatible with this explanation.

## The limits of the word "placebo"

"It's placebo" has become an automatic response in audio discussions. Sometimes it's correct; sometimes it's lazy. Three cautions:

1. **The placebo effect, strictly speaking, is a real subjective improvement.** A person who enjoys music more because of a beautiful cable really is enjoying it more. That doesn't mean the cable changed the sound, but their experience isn't "imaginary" in the sense of nonexistent. The relevant point for a technical discussion is whether **the sound** changes, and that can be tested.
2. **Audible differences sometimes exist where they weren't expected.** A source with high output impedance changes the response of a multi-BA IEM measurably and audibly; 16-bit digital volume without dither can degrade low signals; a laptop output can have noise or interference. Before calling a perception placebo, look for a plausible physical mechanism, and measure.
3. **Absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence**, but the burden of proof grows with how extraordinary the claim is. A claim with no known physical mechanism, which contradicts measurements and doesn't hold up in well-done blind tests, can be treated with strong skepticism, without having to accuse anyone of lying.

<Callout type="chave" title="A balanced position">
Subjective experiences are data about the listener; measurements and blind tests are data about the sound. Both are legitimate and answer different questions. The error lies in using one to answer the other's question: concluding that a cable "improves the stage" from casual listening, or concluding that someone "hears nothing" because "it's all placebo."
</Callout>

## How to protect yourself

- **Match the volume** in any comparison.
- **Compare blind** whenever possible, even simply (another person swaps the equipment).
- **Use short excerpts and quick switches.**
- **Be wary of first impressions** and repeat on another day.
- **Write down in advance what you expect to hear**; afterward, compare with what you heard blind.
- **Look for mechanisms**: if something changes the sound, it must change the pressure at the eardrum, and that can be measured.
