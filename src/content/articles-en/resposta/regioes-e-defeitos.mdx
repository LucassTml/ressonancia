Headphone reviews use a vocabulary of their own: boomy, muddy, shouty, sibilant, veiled, airy. These words aren't empty poetry: most of them correspond, with good regularity, to specific frequency response deviations. Learning the map between words and curves is what lets you read a review and imagine the graph, or read a graph and imagine the sound.
</Lead>

## Bass: sub-bass and mid-bass

A headphone's bass is usually described by two quantities: **how much** there is (the level of the shelf relative to the mids) and **where** it starts and ends.

**Sub-bass** (20–60 Hz) is depth: the "rumble" under a synthesizer note, the sensation of pressure. Well-sealed IEMs reproduce this region easily, because they operate as a pressure chamber (see [transducers](/drivers/transdutores)). When a review says an IEM "has no bass extension," the first suspect should be the seal, not the driver.

**Mid-bass** (60–250 Hz) is body and impact. It's the region that defines whether a kick drum sounds like a firm "thud" or a rounded "boom." A concentrated boost here, especially around 100–200 Hz, sounds **boomy**: bass notes seem to resonate and drag.

A crucial distinction is between a **bass shelf** and a **lift that invades the mids**. The Harman curve, for example, uses a shelf that rises below ~200 Hz and is practically flat above 300 Hz. A lift of the same height that only ends at 500–800 Hz sounds very different: warmer, thicker and, through the [upward spread of masking](/percepcao/mascaramento), with less clarity in the mids.

<Callout type="mito" title="&quot;Fast bass&quot; and &quot;slow bass&quot;">
Listeners describe bass as fast or slow, and the impression is real. But in headphones it rarely comes from the speed of the driver: a diaphragm that reproduces 10 kHz has no difficulty at all keeping up with 80 Hz. What changes the impression is the **amount and shape** of the lift. Excessive mid-bass prolongs the audible part of each note's decay and masks the attack of the following notes; bass with a lower shelf, concentrated in the sub-bass, sounds "faster." High distortion in the bass also contributes to the sensation of "loose" bass.
</Callout>

## Lower mids and mids

**Low-mids** (250–800 Hz) are density. In excess, the sound gets **muddy** or **boxy**, as if coming from inside a cabinet. If lacking, it gets **hollow**, with voices and guitars lacking body.

**Mids** (800 Hz–2 kHz) are character and definition. A lift around 1 kHz sounds **honky**, like a voice on the phone. A wide dip here is the hallmark of V-shaped signatures: voices and melodic instruments become **recessed**, behind bass and treble.

## Presence and upper mids

**Upper mids** (2–5 kHz) are the region of pinna gain and greatest hearing sensitivity. The flaws here are the most annoying:

- **Shouty**: excess around 1.5–3 kHz. Voices and guitars "in your face," aggressive. Quickly tiring at high volume.
- **Harsh** or **strident**: excess at 3–5 kHz, often accompanied by distortion. Rough violins, brass that hurts.
- **Veiled** or **dark**: a lack of energy in this region. The sound seems to come from far away, from behind a curtain.

Since the region is very sensitive, differences of 2 or 3 dB here change the whole impression of the headphone.

## Treble and "air"

**Treble** (5–10 kHz): brightness, cymbals, sibilants.

- **Sibilant**: peaks (usually narrow) between ~5 and 9 kHz that exaggerate "s," "sh" and "z." Sibilance depends greatly on the listener, because the region coincides with the resonance of the occluded canal, which changes with anatomy and insertion.
- **Metallic**, **splashy cymbals**: broader excess at 6–10 kHz.
- **Muffled**: a lack of treble.

**Air** (10–20 kHz): openness and a sensation of space. Headphones with good extension in this region are described as **airy**. Narrow peaks here tend to sound like fine roughness or artificial shimmer.

## Terms that aren't about frequency response

Not all subjective vocabulary reduces to frequency response, or at least not simply.

- **Detail and resolution** are usually associated with more energy at 2–10 kHz, no masking from excessive bass and low distortion. They're largely explained by FR and distortion, but the relationship is indirect.
- **Separation and imaging** also depend on channel matching and the balance between regions. Discussed in [separation and resolution](/iems/separacao-e-resolucao).
- **Soundstage** is the impression of space; in headphones it's influenced by treble, resonances and the recording, as explained in [spatial localization](/percepcao/localizacao-espacial).
- **"Natural"** or "organic" **timbre** generally refers to the balance between the 1–5 kHz harmonics and the fundamental; it's closely tied to the presence region.
- **Musicality** and **warmth** are, in most uses, descriptions of warmer signatures (more bass and mid-bass, less treble), sometimes with a component of second-order distortion.

<Callout type="nota">
The vocabulary isn't standardized, and different communities use the same words in different ways. Academic sensory evaluation literature (for example, the lexicon by Pedersen and Zacharov, 2015) tries to define attributes reproducibly. In practice, the most useful thing is to calibrate each reviewer's vocabulary by comparing their descriptions with measurements of the headphones they reviewed.
</Callout>

With the vocabulary in hand, we can look at the typical combinations of deviations that recur so often they've earned names: [sound signatures](/resposta/assinaturas-sonoras). First, though, we have to answer the most important question: deviations **from what**? That's the subject of [target curves](/resposta/targets-e-harman).
