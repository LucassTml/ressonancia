## Dynamic range compression

A **compressor** monitors the signal's level and reduces the gain when it exceeds a **threshold**. The parameters:

- **Ratio**: above the threshold, how many input decibels correspond to 1 dB of output. At 4:1, a signal 8 dB above the threshold leaves only 2 dB above.
- **Knee**: the smoothness of the transition around the threshold.
- **Attack**: how fast gain reduction begins when the signal rises.
- **Release**: how fast the gain returns when the signal falls.
- **Makeup gain**: recovers the lost volume.

A **limiter** is a compressor with a very high ratio and very fast attack: it keeps the signal from going past a ceiling.

<Lab id="dynamics" />

Compression is indispensable in production and broadcasting (keeping voices intelligible, controlling peaks, "gluing" a mix). In **playback** it has legitimate uses, such as listening in a noisy environment without quiet passages vanishing, or at night without annoying peaks, but it alters the music: it reduces contrasts and, with fast attacks, flattens transients. Overdone, it produces audible "pumping" and was the engine of the [loudness war](/digital/loudness-e-resampling).

<Callout type="nota">
The lab on this site uses a fixed limiter on the master output, with its threshold at −3 dBFS, as protection. It almost never acts with test sounds, but it exists so that no experiment produces unexpected peaks.
</Callout>

## Distortion as an effect

Distortion is, by definition, the addition of components that weren't in the signal. In high-fidelity reproduction, it's something to avoid. As an effect, it's a central creative tool: tape and tube saturation, guitar pedals, "exciters" that add treble harmonics. The [distortion lab](/eletronica/valvulas-vs-transistores) shows how different transfer curves produce different harmonic profiles.

Some sound "enhancement" processors in headphones and phones apply light saturation, harmonic bass enhancement (which exploits the [missing fundamental](/fundamentos/melodia-e-harmonia) effect) or multiband compression. They can sound impressive on first listen, and tiring afterward.

## EQ isn't spatialization

EQ changes the balance of frequencies in each channel. **Spatialization** changes the relationship between channels and the perception of position. They're different things, though they mix: raising the treble can give the impression of more "space," and many spatial processors include EQ.

### Crossfeed

With speakers, each ear also hears the speaker on the opposite side, slightly delayed (~0.3 ms) and with less treble (from the head's shadow). With headphones, that doesn't happen: the left channel goes only to the left ear. The result is an "over-extended" and tiring image on recordings with extreme panning; many recordings from the 1950s and 1960s put whole instruments in one channel.

**Crossfeed**, proposed by Benjamin Bauer in 1961, mixes into each channel a filtered, delayed copy of the other, imitating that condition.

<Lab id="crossfeed" />

Crossfeed doesn't externalize the sound, and the image stays inside the head, but it makes it more cohesive and less fatiguing. It's a matter of preference: many people dislike it and prefer full separation.

### Spatializers and HRTF

More ambitious processors simulate virtual speakers: they apply to each channel the **HRTF** corresponding to a speaker at ±30°, often with room reverberation and, on some systems, **head tracking** (the sound scene stays still when you turn your head). With personalized HRTFs and tracking, externalization can be convincing. With generic HRTFs, results vary a lot between people: for some the sound "leaves the head"; for others it just gets colored. The subject is covered in [spatial localization](/percepcao/localizacao-espacial).

## Which tool for which problem

| Problem | Suitable tool |
| --- | --- |
| Too much or too little bass, sibilance, harshness | EQ |
| Inaudible quiet passages on a bus | Light compression, or better seal/ANC |
| Tiring extreme panning | Crossfeed |
| Sound "inside the head" | Spatialization with HRTF (variable results) |
| Songs at very different volumes | Loudness normalization (ReplayGain) |
| Distortion or hiss | No processing: fix it at the source or the headphone |
