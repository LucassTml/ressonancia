<Lead>
On a forum, one person says an IEM is bright and sibilant; another, that it's dark and muffled; a third, that it's perfectly balanced. It's tempting to conclude someone is lying or can't hear. Most of the time, they're all honestly describing different sounds, because the same headphone produces different sounds in different ears.
</Lead>

<Callout type="simples">
The same earphone can really sound different to two people, because the fit, the ear canal and the ears themselves are different. The seal changes the bass, how deep it goes changes the treble, and age and habit change the rest. So when reviews disagree, nobody has to be wrong.
</Callout>

## The same curve, different eardrums

A measurement on a coupler shows the pressure the headphone produces in a **standardized** ear. What reaches your eardrum depends on your ear. The differences come from several physical sources, each dominating a range of frequencies.

### Seal: the bass

<Diagram id="seal" />

In an IEM, bass depends on a closed chamber between the earphone and the eardrum. Any leak, whether an eartip that's too small, an oval canal or a jaw movement when talking or chewing, creates an air outlet and turns the chamber into a leaky system that behaves like a high-pass filter. A small leak, imperceptible to the touch, already reduces the sub-bass by several decibels; a moderate leak can remove 10 dB or more at 30 Hz.

This is by far the most common factor in disagreements about bass. When one person says an IEM "has no bass" and another says it has too much, the first hypothesis to test is the seal. The lab below lets you see and hear the effect.

### Insertion depth: the treble

As you saw in [pinna gain](/percepcao/pinna-gain), the canal occluded by an IEM has a half-wave resonance that depends on the length of the residual canal. Inserting the IEM deeper shortens that length and shifts the resonance up; inserting it more shallowly shifts it down. Since ear canals have different lengths and people insert differently, the peak (or dip) that appears around 7–10 kHz in measurements moves from person to person, and sometimes from one day to the next in the same person.

That's why disagreements about sibilance and brightness concentrate in the 5 to 10 kHz region. A peak at 8 kHz that lands right on your canal's resonance can sound aggressive to you and go unnoticed by another listener.

### Canal geometry and eardrum impedance

Longer or wider canals, more or less curved, and eardrums with different impedances alter the response in ways no standardized coupler fully captures. The IEC 60318-4 coupler represents one average; the B&K 5128 represents another average, with a more realistic canal. Neither is you.

### Pinna and head (in headphones)

With over-ear headphones, the size and shape of the ear, the distance between driver and ear and the placement of the pad change the acoustic interaction, especially above 2 kHz. Moving a headphone a few millimeters on your head can change the treble by several decibels.

## Differences in hearing itself

Besides the acoustics, there are differences in the auditory system itself.

**Age.** Presbycusis starts with the treble and advances with time. At 60, a loss of 20 to 30 dB above 4 kHz is common (ISO 7029). People who've lost treble sensitivity tend to prefer brighter headphones and find "dark" those that younger people consider balanced.

**History of noise exposure.** Noise-induced loss usually appears as a "notch" around 4 kHz (sometimes between 3 and 6 kHz). It alters perception right in the most sensitive region and the one most important for presence.

**Training.** Trained listeners notice smaller differences and describe what they hear more consistently. Research by Sean Olive with loudspeakers showed that trained and untrained listeners tend to prefer the same things, but trained listeners are far more consistent and discriminate better.

**Adaptation.** The brain adapts to what it hears often. After weeks with a very dark headphone, a neutral one can seem bright for a while, until the internal reference readjusts. Part of what is attributed to headphone *burn-in* is probably listener adaptation.

## Differences in preference

Even if two listeners received exactly the same pressure at the eardrum, they could prefer different things. Harman's research on target curves showed that the average preference for the Harman curve hides **groups** of listeners: a majority who like something close to the curve, a group who want more bass and a smaller group who want less. The studies found associations with age (younger listeners tending to prefer more bass and treble), gender and experience; these are statistical trends that don't determine the preference of any specific person.

Musical genre matters too: electronic music and hip-hop are usually mastered with intense bass, and what sounds balanced with acoustic jazz can sound heavy with them, and vice versa.

<Callout type="preferencia">
It's legitimate to like a headphone that's warmer, brighter or more V-shaped than a target curve suggests. Target curves describe average trends, not obligations. What isn't legitimate is treating preference as fact ("this headphone is bad because it has too much bass") without saying compared to what and for whom.
</Callout>

## How to deal with all this in practice

1. **Find the right seal before judging.** Try several sizes and types of eartips. A simple test: play music with constant bass and press lightly on the IEM; if the bass increases a lot, the seal wasn't good.
2. **Use measurements as a starting point, not a verdict.** A graph gives reliable trends below ~5 kHz. Above that, treat it as indicative.
3. **Find your own curve.** Instead of relying on others' perception, adjust a headphone with EQ until it's good for you, and compare with measurements. The method is in [how to find your preference curve](/resposta/sua-curva).
4. **Read opinions by identifying the reviewer's reference.** Someone who considers a Harman headphone "dark" probably prefers more treble, or has a different insertion. Opinions are useful when you know how to calibrate them.
5. **Be wary of consensus about treble and soundstage.** They're the attributes most dependent on individual anatomy.
