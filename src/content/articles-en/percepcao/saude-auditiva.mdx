## How sound damages hearing

Very intense sounds cause damage by two main mechanisms. At extreme levels (explosions, gunshots, above ~130–140 dB SPL peak), the damage is **mechanical**: structures in the organ of Corti rupture. At sustained high levels, those that matter for headphone users, the damage is mainly **metabolic**: hair cells, especially outer ones, work at their limit, accumulate oxidative stress and, with repeated exposure, die.

The first cells to suffer are those in the region of the cochlea that responds to ~3–6 kHz. That's why noise-induced loss typically appears as a "notch" on the audiogram around 4 kHz, right in the region of greatest sensitivity and most importance for presence and intelligibility.

### Temporary loss, permanent loss and "hidden loss"

After a concert or hours of headphones at high volume, it's common for ears to feel "muffled" or to ring. That's a **temporary threshold shift** (TTS): sensitivity drops and recovers in hours or days. For a long time, it was believed that a TTS that fully recovered left no aftereffects.

Research by Sharon Kujawa and Charles Liberman, from 2009, showed in animals that exposures causing fully reversible TTS can permanently destroy a portion of the **synapses** between inner hair cells and auditory nerve fibers, without changing the audiogram. This "cochlear synaptopathy," or hidden hearing loss, can impair understanding in noisy environments and the resolution of detail, even with normal thresholds. Its extent in humans is still being studied, but the message is one of caution: the absence of loss on the audiogram doesn't guarantee an absence of damage.

### Tinnitus

**Tinnitus** is the perception of sound without an external stimulus: a whistle, hiss or noise. It's often associated with cochlear damage, including from sound exposure, and can be temporary (after a concert) or permanent. Persistent tinnitus merits a medical evaluation.

## Dose: level × time

The risk depends on accumulated sound energy. Since energy doubles every +3 dB, standards use a **3 dB exchange rule**: every 3 dB more, half the permitted time.

- **NIOSH (USA, occupational health, 1998):** 85 dB(A) for 8 hours a day. At 88 dB(A), 4 hours; at 94 dB(A), 1 hour; at 100 dB(A), 15 minutes.
- **WHO/ITU H.870 (2019, safe listening on personal devices):** 80 dB(A) for 40 hours **per week** for adults and 75 dB(A) for children, with the same 3 dB rule.

These criteria are statistical: they define a risk considered acceptable for the population, not a threshold below which nothing happens to anyone. People differ in susceptibility.

## How much does a headphone produce?

In practice, it's hard to know the exact level at the eardrum. Some references help:

- Comfortable listening in a quiet environment usually falls between 65 and 80 dB(A) average level.
- Many phones and sources can drive sensitive IEMs well above 100 dB SPL. The [power calculator](/eletronica/potencia-e-sensibilidade) shows how much a headphone produces at a given voltage.
- Modern smartphones (iOS and Android) estimate headphone exposure and issue alerts based on WHO/ITU criteria, with reasonable precision for the manufacturer's own headphones and approximate for others.
- In noisy environments, the tendency is to raise the volume 10 to 15 dB to overcome the noise. It's the biggest risk factor in everyday use.

## How to listen a lot for many years

1. **Reduce the noise rather than raising the music.** Well-sealed IEMs isolate 20–30 dB in the mids and treble; active cancellation helps in the bass. With less noise, the same detail appears with less volume. This is perhaps the greatest health advantage of a good IEM.
2. **Set the volume in a quiet environment, at the start of the session,** and avoid raising it over time to compensate for fatigue.
3. **Take breaks.** The ear recovers better with intervals.
4. **Watch for warning signs:** ringing, muffled ears, a sensation of "pressure" after listening. They're signs the dose was high.
5. **Use protection at concerts and in very noisy places.** "Flat" attenuation earplugs (musicians' earplugs) lower the level while keeping a reasonable tonal balance.
6. **Get periodic audiometry** if you listen a lot, play instruments or work with sound.
